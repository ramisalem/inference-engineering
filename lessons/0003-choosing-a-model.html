The most important decision

Baseten's book calls model selection "the most important decision in model performance optimization". The rule it gives is short: pick "the smallest, easiest-to-run model that's smart enough to handle the task at hand", because "inference on a smaller model with fewer parameters will be faster and cheaper than inference on a larger model".Baseten, Inference Engineering, §1.3 "Model selection". Every later optimization (quantization, batching, speculative decoding) makes a model faster. None of them can make a too-big model small. As an app developer you already think this way about databases: you don't pick Postgres or DynamoDB from a leaderboard, you pick from your access patterns, then benchmark with your own queries.

A model choice has four parts:

  1. Who runs it? Open or closed weights, on shared or dedicated hardware.
  2. Is it smart enough? Only your own eval can tell you.
  3. Is it fast and cheap enough? Latency from Lesson 2, and cost per request.
  4. If not, what then? A bigger model, thinking, a better prompt, fine-tuning or distillation.

Open vs closed weights

A closed model's weights stay with its maker. You can only use it through the maker's API, "closed models like GPT and Gemini" in the book's words.Book §1.3 again, where it notes you can start out on pay-per-token APIs for "even closed models like GPT and Gemini". Anthropic's Claude models are closed in the same way. An open-weight model's weights can be downloaded, so you can run them anywhere: on your Mac, on rented GPUs, or through someone else's API. Both of today's models are open, but they come with different licenses:

  • Qwen3 is open-weighted "under Apache 2.0 license", the same license as many libraries you already depend on.Qwen team, "Qwen3: Think Deeper, Act Faster". Six dense sizes: 0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B and 32B parameters.
  • Llama 3.2 uses Meta's own community license. If you ship it, you must "prominently display 'Built with Llama'", and a company with more than "700 million monthly active users" on the release date must ask Meta for a license first.Meta, Llama 3.2 Community License, §1.b.i and §2 "Additional Commercial Terms". Models trained from Llama outputs must also start their name with "Llama".

So "open" doesn't mean "no strings". Read a model's license the way you'd read a dependency's.

That last row is why an inference engineer's work is mostly with open models. The book adds a practical tip: "Stick with popular model architectures to ensure you'll find robust support across the performance tooling landscape." vLLM, llama.cpp and MLX all support Llama and Qwen on day one. A niche architecture may wait months for the same support.

Shared vs dedicated inference

Shared inference means many customers send requests to the same deployment, and each pays per million tokens. Some companies "will sell consumption directly via a public shared inference API".Baseten, Inference Engineering, §1.2 "About your app". Dedicated inference means GPUs reserved for you, paid by the hour whether they're busy or idle. The book warns that "cost accounting gets messy in the transition from paying per million tokens to paying directly for infrastructure."Book, chapter 7 "Production", introduction.

Its advice for early products is blunt: "Before product-market fit, it's not worth spending time or money on doing your own inference." Dedicated starts to pay off once usage is large and steady. The napkin math:

break-even tokens/month = dedicated cost per month ÷ shared price per token

There's a catch Lesson 2 prepared you for. The dedicated GPU must actually serve those tokens at your latency target, which means enough throughput, and that's a point on your latency–throughput curve.

Who the users are changes the answer. The book: "Consumer applications are generally much more cost-sensitive and have less predictable usage patterns", while "Business-to-business products often have better margins and more stable usage" and "Inference engineers building for businesses must favor latency and uptime".Book §1.2.3 "Consumer versus B2B". Spiky consumer traffic leaves a dedicated GPU idle much of the day, so shared or autoscaled capacity usually wins there. In an AI-native app, where the model is the product, inference is a cost on every single request. That's why these sums matter so much: "Generative AI models have unlocked a new class of applications across industries and domains", and "each category of application relies on different models and modalities and requires tailored inference."

Reasoning models: paying in tokens for quality

A reasoning model writes out its thinking before it answers. Qwen3 can do both. In thinking mode "the model takes time to reason step by step before delivering the final answer", and in non-thinking mode it gives "quick, near-instant responses". Qwen reports "scalable and smooth performance improvements that are directly correlated with the computational reasoning budget allocated".Qwen3 blog (above), "Hybrid Thinking Modes". The blog documents /think and /no_think prompt switches. On your Ollama (0.40) they didn't work in testing, so today's tool sends reasoning_effort: "none" instead. That's the +nothink suffix.

For an inference engineer, a thought token costs the same as an answer token. Each one is decoded at the ITL you measured, holds a slot while it's generated, and is billed as output. There's also a measurement trap: TTFT counts the first token of any kind. A thinking model streams its first thought quickly, so its TTFT looks great while the user is still waiting for an answer. For reasoning models, also track the time to the first answer token.

Evals: unit tests for a model

"Model evaluation, or evals, is the practice of systematically measuring model intelligence." Public benchmarks help you make a shortlist, "but they have become saturated or even gamed", and "there is no substitute for directly measuring how a model performs for your application."Book §1.3.1 "Model evaluation". For shortlisting, Artificial Analysis publishes quality, speed and price comparisons and explains how it measures them.

Hamel Husain's framing will feel familiar: "Unit tests for LLMs are assertions (like you would write in pytest)." He describes three levels: unit tests, human and model review, and A/B tests in production, where "The cost of Level 3 > Level 2 > Level 1."Hamel Husain, "Your AI Product Needs Evals". Also: "You must remove all friction from the process of looking at data." Today's tool is Level 1. Each task has a prompt and some automatic checks, like "the answer is exactly payment" or "the JSON has order_id 40177". A task passes only if all its checks pass. The tool prints every failing answer too, because the pass rate tells you that a model fails, and only the answers tell you why.

Two cautions. Twelve tasks is a smoke test, not a verdict: each task is worth 8.3 points, so a one-task gap is noise. And run at temperature 0 with a fixed seed. The teacher's two runs of today's eval gave the same pass/fail results task for task.

Fine-tuning is how you keep the model small: "If you can fine-tune a small model to pass your evals, you set yourself up for an easier time" serving it. Distillation goes further, because it "shows the student model the teacher model's actual probability distributions, not just its final answers". It "sees substantially less real-world use than fine-tuning", but there are famous examples. DeepSeek's small distilled models "showed similar reasoning behavior to the main DeepSeek-R1 model".Book §1.3.2 "Fine-tuning" and §1.3.3 "Distillation". You'll do neither in this course; they're on the roadmap so you can reason about them in a design discussion.

Exercises

From memory first.

A two-person startup is adding an AI feature and hasn't found product-market fit yet. Where should it run inference?

  • On a shared API, paying for each token it uses
  • On dedicated GPUs, so that its monthly bill stays fixed
  • On its own servers, so it learns inference early on
  • On a fine-tuned model, so quality beats the big APIs
The book: "Before product-market fit, it's not worth spending time or money on doing your own inference." Shared APIs mean no idle GPUs and no operations work. Look again once usage is large and steady (the break-even below), or when latency, privacy or customization needs force the move.

Model A tops a public benchmark. Model B scores slightly lower, is half the size and is cheaper to run. What should decide between them?

  • A test set built from your own app's real tasks
  • The leaderboard rank, since it tests many tasks at once
  • The model with more parameters, since bigger is always smarter
  • The newer of the two, since newer releases are better
Benchmarks are for shortlisting. They "have become saturated or even gamed", and "there is no substitute for directly measuring how a model performs for your application." Today, the 3B llama3.2 beat the 1.7B qwen3 with thinking off, and lost to the same 1.7B with thinking on. Size alone didn't predict the ranking.

Your dashboard shows a reasoning model's TTFT p50 at 87 ms, but users say answers take about 4 seconds. What explains the gap?

  • The first streamed tokens are thinking, not the answer
  • The dashboard measures TTFT on the server, not client
  • The answers are long, so decoding takes the time
  • The requests queue on the server before any prefill
TTFT measures the first token of any kind. A thinking model streams its first thought almost at once, then spends seconds thinking before the answer starts. Today's answers were one word long, yet E2E was ~3.9 s, because ~300 tokens of thinking came first. Track the time to the first answer token separately.

Your (imaginary) app already had 900 million monthly active users when both models were released. Which one can you ship without asking its maker first?

  • Qwen3, released under the Apache 2.0 license
  • Llama 3.2, released under its community license
  • Either, since open weights have no terms
  • Neither, since open weights ban commercial use
The Llama 3.2 license says a licensee with "greater than 700 million monthly active users" on the release date "must request a license from Meta". Apache 2.0 has no such clause. Open weights are not the same as no terms, so read the license as you would a dependency's.

In your eval, llama3.2 answered 45 to the delivery-shift question (the right answer is 21). What should you do first?

  • Read the answer and decide: model, prompt or check
  • Switch to a bigger model and run every task
  • Delete the task, since one failure is only noise
  • Raise the temperature so the model tries other answers
Look at the data first: "You must remove all friction from the process of looking at data." Here it's the model (7.5 h − 0.5 h = 420 min ÷ 20 = 21). With your own tasks it's often the check (Late delivery isn't late_delivery) or the prompt. The fix is different for each.

What does distillation give a small model that ordinary fine-tuning on the big model's answers doesn't?

  • The teacher's probabilities for every token, not just answers
  • The teacher's weights copied directly into the smaller model
  • The teacher's thinking text, shown before every final answer
  • The teacher's hardware, so the student runs equally fast
Distillation "shows the student model the teacher model's actual probability distributions, not just its final answers". For each token, the student learns how likely the teacher thought every option was, not just which one it picked. It's used less than fine-tuning because it needs the teacher running alongside.

A reasoning model reads 300 input tokens and writes 300 thinking tokens plus a 20-token answer. Prices: $0.10 per million input tokens, $0.40 per million output tokens. What do 1,000 requests cost?

Per request: 300 × $0.10/1M + 320 × $0.40/1M = $0.00003 + $0.000128 = $0.000158. Times 1,000 = $0.158. Without thinking it would be 300 × 0.10 + 20 × 0.40 = $0.038 per 1,000, about 4× less. Thinking tokens are billed as output, which usually costs more per token than input.

Imaginary prices: a dedicated GPU costs $2 per hour, around the clock (30-day month). A shared API charges $0.50 per million tokens. How many tokens per month must you serve before dedicated is cheaper?

Dedicated: $2 × 24 × 30 = $1,440 a month. Break-even: $1,440 ÷ $0.50 per million = 2,880 million tokens (2.9 billion) a month. Below that, shared is cheaper. This ignores the engineering time to run your own deployment, which pushes the real break-even higher.

Same GPU. To reach that break-even, what average throughput must it serve, every second of the month?

A 30-day month is 30 × 86,400 = 2,592,000 s. 2.88 billion ÷ 2.592 million ≈ 1,111 tokens/s, day and night. Your Mac did ~50–75. Spiky consumer traffic makes it harder: at 3 a.m. the GPU is idle but still billed. That's why the break-even question is really a question about your latency–throughput curve and your traffic shape.
